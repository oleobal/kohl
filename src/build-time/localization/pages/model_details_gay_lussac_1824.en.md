The first "modern" alcoholmetry model.

### History

Gay-Lussac was tasked with the French government to improve measurements of alcohol content in liquids for tax purposes in the early nineteenth century. At that point it had been already noted that density lowered with alcohol content, with practical application through the Baumé and Cartier alcoholmeters.

Gay-Lussac's innovation was to relate not just an arbitrary measurement but actual physical quantities together (ABV, density, temperature), and bring a genuine scientific rigor to the process. He also drove improvements in instrumentation through the adoption of glass rather than metal instruments.

However, he never published his method or results! Rather, he associated with a man named Collareau to sell alcoholmeters, along with an instruction manual<sup>GL24</sup> that serves as the main source for all this. He did submit his research to the Académie des sciences,<sup>AdS22</sup> which provided the backing to get his instrument adopted as law.<sup>JO24</sup> Presumably the density tables were kept secret as to prevent competitors in the sale of instruments.

Much drama ensued. In 1845 Pierre contradicted Gay-Lussac in his measurement of pure alcohol density. This sparked a debate in which Gay-Lussac's data was reconstructed, by Pouillet<sup>Po59</sup> and Ruau,<sup>Ru61</sup> which pushed Collardeau to publish the original 1824 table.. but only at 15°C.<sup>Co61</sup> Today the actual, original tables are presumably in Gay-Lussac's papers, which haven't been made available online.

### Scentific value and reconstruction

Based on the _Séance_<sup>AdS22</sup>, it seems Gay-Lussac took density readings every 5 degrees from 0 to 30°C and every 5%<sub>vol</sub> from 0 to 100 (6×21 = 126 values). All other values were interpolated.

The most notable flaw in the Gay-Lussac model (aside from its unpublished state) is how it assumes that, at 15°C, water has a density of 1000g/L and alcohol 794.7g/L. This alcohol value was sensible for the time and could be reproduced. However the water value was wrong, even in 1820: the many issues with defining the kilogram in relation to a volume of water had been addressed twenty years prior with the _kilogramme des archives_, and even then it was known that the maximum density of water was at 4 rather than 15°C. Indeed it's hard to imagine Gay-Lussac making this mistake, and possibly it was made by whomever wrote the _Séance_.<sup>AdS22</sup>

I reconstructed the densities through matching the apparent ABV values from the _table de la force réelle_ in _Instruction_<sup>GL24</sup> to the Collareau densities, as in the Pouillet paper. The table provides no values for 0 and 100 ABV so I interpolated them from adjacent values. We don't know what instruments or precise protocol were used, however I assumed glass instruments calibrated at 15°C.
